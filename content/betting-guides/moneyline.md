A moneyline is the simplest bet there is: pick who wins. On a first-segment moneyline you're picking who's ahead after the first period, quarter, half, inning or five innings. What happens after that doesn't matter.

## How the price works

- **Minus is the favorite.** -150 means you risk $150 to win $100.
- **Plus is the underdog.** +150 means you risk $100 to win $150.

Two ways to look at the same price:

| Price | Risk $100, you win | To win $100, you risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -150 | $67 | $150 |
| -110 | $91 | $110 |
| +150 | $150 | $67 |

Bet $110 on a team at -110 and it wins: you get back $210, your $110 plus $100 profit.

## The tie

This is the big difference from a full game moneyline. First segments end tied all the time, so it matters how your bet handles it. Think soccer: Team A, Team B or the draw. Something has to win.

- **2-way moneyline:** a tie is a push. You get your money back. You can't lose on a tie, and that's an advantage.
- **3-way moneyline:** the tie is its own bet. If it's level, Team A and Team B both lose. [3-way explained](/betting-guides/3-way-moneyline/)

## When the moneyline beats the spread

When the first-segment spread is only half a point, the moneyline and the spread are almost the same bet. Compare the prices and take the better one. If the favorite is a big price, the spread or the 3-way can be the better way in.

## FAQ

### What happens to a first quarter moneyline bet if the quarter ends tied?
On a 2-way moneyline it's a push and you get your money back.

### What does -150 mean?
You bet $150 to win $100. The minus side is the favorite.

### Is a first period moneyline the same as a 3-way?
No. A 2-way pushes on a tie. A 3-way makes the tie its own bet, and Team A and Team B both lose if it's level.
